The hide is a plywood box the size of a garden shed, with a bunk, a bucket and a slit to look through. You walk in at four in the afternoon and you do not leave until eight the next morning, because the bears can smell a door opening from across the bog.

I had booked three nights. For the first two I photographed ravens, snow, and the inside of my own sleeping bag.

![Spruce forest disappearing into thick fog](/images/opus-form-YrSy6IGlRMY-unsplash.jpg "The view on the second morning. Somewhere in there, apparently, were eleven bears. 35 mm, f/8, 1/60 s.")

## What sixty-one hours buy you

Wildlife photography is mostly logistics and thermal underwear. The picture at the top of this page took a thousandth of a second. Getting to that thousandth took a flight, six hours of gravel road and a guide called Lassi who has been feeding salmon to the same family of bears [since 1998](/about.html).

On the third evening the wind turned and the snow came in sideways. I had written the trip off when a male walked out of the treeline, looked at the weather, and lay down forty metres from the slit.

> He put his head on his paws like a dog that has been told to wait, and for two hours neither of us moved.

## The four that worked

I pressed the shutter 2,300 times in three nights. Most of those frames show snow in front of a bear, a bear behind snow, or a very sharp branch. Four are worth printing. That ratio sounds terrible and is about average.

If you are thinking about a hide of your own: go in late autumn, bring a second battery for every hour of darkness, and do not drink the whole thermos before midnight.
